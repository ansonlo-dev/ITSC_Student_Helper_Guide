# Talking to Users

Most complaints about IT support are about communication, not technical skill.

## Greeting

- Look up, make eye contact, and greet within a few seconds of someone arriving.
- If you are mid-task: "I'll be with you in about two minutes" — then keep to it.
- Ask an open question: "How can I help?" rather than "Do you have a problem?"

## Language

- Use the language the user starts in — English, Cantonese, or Mandarin — and
  switch if they seem more comfortable in another.
- Avoid jargon. "Your password has expired" is clearer than "your credential TTL
  lapsed".
- Explain in terms of what the user must **do**, not what the system is doing.

## While you work

- Narrate briefly: "I'm going to restart this, it'll take about a minute."
- Ask before touching their laptop, and ask before closing anything.
- If you need to look at their screen, ask them to close anything private first.
- Silence makes people anxious. A short update every minute or so is enough.

## Explaining the fix

End every interaction with:

1. What was wrong, in one sentence.
2. What you did.
3. What they should do if it happens again.

## Saying no

You will often have to decline. Do it clearly, kindly, and with a next step:

> "I'm not able to reset a password without seeing your student card — that rule
> protects your account. If you bring it to the counter, we can do it right away."

> "That one is beyond what I'm allowed to change, but I'll pass it to our
> technician and they'll follow up with you."

Do not apologise repeatedly, invent a reason, or blame a colleague or "the
system". State the limit, then offer the path forward.

## When a user is upset

::: tip De-escalation
1. Let them finish. Do not interrupt or defend.
2. Acknowledge the impact: "I understand, that deadline is today — that's stressful."
3. Restate the problem so they know you heard it.
4. Say what you *can* do right now, and be specific about timing.
5. If they stay angry or want to complain, call a full-time staff member. That is
   the correct action, not a last resort.
:::

Never argue, never raise your voice, and never take it personally. If you feel
unsafe, step away and get staff immediately.

## Following up

If you promised a call-back, make sure it happens — either by you or by whoever
takes over. An unkept promise costs more goodwill than the original fault.
